  First question: How should these notes be generated?

  - A) Claude API at generation time — call Claude during KB generation to write each note
  based on the POI's type, class, name, system, and description. Highest quality, most
  varied, but requires API access and costs tokens. ~340 planets + hundreds of other POIs.
  - B) Template-based with randomized phrases — hand-write 5-10 template paragraphs per POI
  type with variable slots filled from the data. Consistent tone, no API dependency, but
  less varied and more repetitive.
  - C) Pre-generate a batch with Claude, store in a JSON/DB file — generate all notes once
  via Claude, save them, then the KB generator just reads from the file. Best of both
  worlds: high quality notes, no API calls during normal builds, easy to regenerate
  selectively.

  I'd recommend C — you get Claude's creativity for the notes, but they're stable artifacts
  that don't change every build. You can regenerate individual notes if they're not great,
  and the KB generator just reads the file.

⎿  --- Haven Star (sun/G5V) in  ---
     Spotted Haven Star from three parsecs out—a perfectly ordinary yellow dwarf going
     about its business, unremarkable in every way except that it's managed to sustain
     seventeen inhabited worlds without incident. After two centuries surveying the
     galaxy's more temperamental celestial furnaces, there's something almost restful
     about a star that simply *works*, though I'll admit the name feels like wishful
     thinking from whoever settled here first. Still, I'd take a reliable G5V over
     another blue giant waiting to go supernova any day of the week.

     --- 82 Eridani Star (sun/G8V) in 82 Eridani ---
     The star itself cuts a rather humble profile—a G8V, nothing that would turn heads
     at the Academy, though I suppose that's precisely what makes it a reliable anchor
     for the three terrestrial worlds orbiting it. I've catalogued dimmer suns before,
     but there's something almost companionable about a star this modest; it reminds me
      of the yellow dwarf near Kepler Station where I spent those two quiet months in
     '47, though this one burns with marginally less vigor. Still, after six centuries
     of stellar surveys, I've learned that the quiet ones often harbor the most
     interesting secrets in their planetary systems—so I'll be making a full circuit
     before moving on.

     --- Haven Prime (planet/super_terran) in  ---
     The readings confirmed what the settlement beacons promised—Haven Prime delivers
     on its reputation, a verdant super-terran with oxygen-rich atmosphere and
     genuinely temperate zones that don't require respirators or environmental suits.
     I've catalogued six distinct biomes in a single continental sweep, each with flora
      that rivals the engineered gardens of the outer colonies, though mercifully less
     pretentious about it. Still, after two centuries of bureaucratic expansion, the
     place feels less like a frontier discovery and more like a well-appointed resort
     with excellent groundwater reserves. Magnificent world, yes—but I confess I'm more
      eager to backtrack into the Kepler Expanse where the gravity wells still surprise
      you.

     --- 82 Eridani I (planet/tundra) in 82 Eridani ---
     The ice world of 82 Eridani I lives up to its reputation—a pristine tundra locked
     in perpetual winter, with methane deposits glinting beneath sheets of frozen
     nitrogen that could swallow a starship whole. I've walked colder worlds, but never
      one so *quiet*; the atmospheric density swallows sound, leaving only the whisper
     of wind against the hab-suit. The geological surveys will love the mineral
     abundance here, though I confess I found myself more transfixed by the aurora-like
      refractions dancing across the ice shelves at dusk—a reminder that even in the
     desolate corners of the galaxy, there's beauty in the barren. Worth a return visit
      if the colony proposals ever get funded; someone should witness this place before
      drilling operations turn it into just another resource node.

     --- Federation Vaults (station/) in  ---
     The Federation Vaults live up to their reputation—all gleaming titanium corridors
     and security checkpoints that make even a veteran feel like a smuggler. I've seen
     three of these bureaucratic monuments now, and they're as interchangeable as they
     come: same austere aesthetic, same air of self-important efficiency, same endless
     forms required just to dock. Still, I'll grant them this much: their archival
     systems are genuinely impressive, and if I ever need to cross-reference something
     from my first survey mission in the Kepler Expanse, at least I know where to find
     it.

     --- Grand Exchange (station/) in  ---
     The Grand Exchange lives up to its reputation as a bureaucratic monument to
     commerce—all gleaming docking bays and efficient queuing systems that somehow
     manage to drain the soul while processing cargo. I've restocked supplies and filed
      the mandatory seventeen reports, each one a small death by data entry. Still,
     I'll admit the observation deck offers a decent view of the surrounding nebula,
     and the station's archivists pulled some fascinating historical records on the
     sector's early colonization that might prove useful for the next survey leg.

     --- Commerce Fields (asteroid_belt/metallic) in  ---
     Passed through the Commerce Fields today—another metallic asteroid belt, though
     I'll admit the iron and nickel concentrations are respectable enough to warrant a
     survey notation. The trade consortium probably has three stations already
     siphoning ore from here, which means I'll file my report, collect my modest
     finder's fee, and move on to somewhere the dust hasn't settled yet. Still, there's
      something oddly satisfying about watching the sunlight catch on a thousand
     metallic surfaces; even routine prospecting has its moments of austere beauty.

     --- 82 Eridani Belt (asteroid_belt/mixed) in 82 Eridani ---
     The 82 Eridani Belt sprawls across the system like someone knocked over a cosmic
     junkyard—yet another mixed assemblage of rock and regolith that'll keep the survey
      drones busy for a week while I file the requisite reports back to Station.
     Nothing here screams discovery, though I suppose the distribution patterns might
     interest the mineralogists enough to justify the fuel burn. Still beats
     cataloguing the trade routes near Sirius; at least the silence out here doesn't
     come with bureaucratic chatter.

     --- Trade Winds (gas_cloud/molecular_cloud) in  ---
     Stumbled into Trade Winds today—a proper stellar nursery, dense with hydrogen and
     helium, the kind of chaotic creative space that makes you remember why you signed
     up for this work in the first place. The protostars embedded within are still
     finding their rhythm, and I caught myself just drifting through the luminous haze
     for twenty minutes like some starry-eyed cadet, watching gravity do its patient
     work. Magnificent reminder that the universe doesn't need to be strange to be
     humbling; sometimes it just needs to be *big*.

     --- Achernar Gas Pocket (gas_cloud/) in Achernar ---
     Found myself drifting through the Achernar Gas Pocket this cycle—a luminous
     cathedral of argon and neon that painted the viewport in shades of violet I
     haven't seen since the Horsehead Nebula back in '87. The compressed hydrogen
     layers create these peculiar density gradients, almost musical in how they refract
      starlight, and there's enough plasma gas churning through the deeper pockets to
     make a prospector's heart sing. Beautiful in the way only a gas cloud can be:
     utterly gorgeous, utterly useless for landing, and absolutely not worth the fuel
     to extract anything substantial. Still, I sat there for an hour just watching the
     colors dance—sometimes the vastness of space deserves more than a scan and a
     heading.

     --- Frostmarket Flats (ice_field/kuiper) in  ---
     The Frostmarket Flats live up to their tedious reputation—endless sheets of water
     ice and methane stretching across the Kuiper dark like a cosmic parking lot. I've
     catalogued three identical survey markers from previous expeditions, each one
     presumably planted by someone equally underwhelmed, and my instruments confirm
     what the name already promised: frozen volatiles, nothing more. Still, there's a
     peculiar beauty in how the methane catches starlight at this distance, painting
     the ice in shades of blue-grey that wouldn't disgrace a proper nebula, even if the
      geologists back home will file this under "routine resources, minimal interest."
     Another tick on the survey log; another reason to appreciate that my next
     assignment lands somewhere with actual mystery.

     --- 82 Eridani Frozen Drift (ice_field/kuiper) in 82 Eridani ---
     Passed through the Frozen Drift today—a sprawling graveyard of water ice and
     methane chunks drifting in the deep cold, unremarkable except for the peculiar
     geometry of some larger fragments, which suggest ancient collision patterns I'd
     swear I've seen before near Proxima. Nothing here to warrant a mining expedition
     or scientific team, but there's a strange comfort in these quiet places, far from
     the chatter of the inner systems, where you can hear your own thoughts beneath the
      solar wind.

     --- Pale Crystal Drift — Botein (nebula/) in Botein ---
     The Pale Crystal Drift lives up to its name—a whisper of a nebula where the energy
      crystals are so anemic they'd barely qualify as a glimmer. I've seen nurseries
     where stellar birth roars across three sectors, but this one feels like watching
     something fade rather than kindle, the charge density too sparse to coax anything
     magnificent from the copper and iron scattered through the dark. Still, there's an
      oddly beautiful melancholy to it, the way those dim crystals catch what little
     light penetrates here—a kind of cosmic afterthought that somehow demands respect.

     --- Cedarhold Dim Lattice (nebula/) in Cedarhold ---
     The Cedarhold Dim Lattice caught me off guard—I'd expected another featureless gas
      cloud, but instead found this delicate tracery of crystalline geometry suspended
     in near-vacuum, each formation barely luminous yet somehow alive with structure.
     The energy signature is faint, almost apologetic, yet there's something humbling
     about witnessing such intricate natural architecture, even at low intensity;
     reminds me of ice cathedrals I saw in the Korvaxian reaches, except these won't
     weep come spring. The Copper Ore and Iron Ore deposits within are substantial
     enough to warrant a prospector's visit, though the true prize seems to be the
     Energy Crystals themselves—fragile-looking things that somehow persist in this
     quiet corner of space. For once, bureaucratic surveying felt justified.

     --- Crimson Signal Outpost Remnants (relic/derelict) in Bharani ---
     Found the skeletal remains of what was once Crimson Signal—a communications relay
     station, judging by the dish arrays still clawing at the void—drifting silent in
     the Bharani system, and I'm struck by how quickly ambition becomes archaeology out
      here. The hull bears scorch marks consistent with a solar flare event, perhaps
     three centuries old; whatever distress signal they sent never found purchase. I've
      seen outposts abandoned before, but there's something unsettling about this
     one—the way the solar panels track an empty sun, still reaching, still hoping.
     Makes the *Meridian's* hum feel very present, very real.

     --- Ruby's Vigil (relic/) in Iron Reach ---
     The rust-eaten sentinel still stands after what must be centuries—some kind of
     monitoring station or memorial, its hull streaked with oxidized iron that gives
     the whole thing a mournful beauty. I found myself circling it for nearly an hour,
     trying to parse the script on its base, though the radiation bloom around it made
     a closer approach inadvisable; whatever Ruby was watching for, she never got the
     relief of standing down. It reminds me of the lighthouse keepers I read about on
     old Earth, tending their lights for ships that might never come—except this one's
     light has been dark for generations, and the only thing passing by now is me.
